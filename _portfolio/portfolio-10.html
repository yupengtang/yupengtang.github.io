Many data-driven microclimate models forecast a single step ahead, at locations that already have sensor history. The harder and more useful question is how well you can forecast hours ahead at a location that has no sensor at all. I built an end-to-end ML system for that setting on the Georgia Tech campus, along with the benchmark used to decide which models belong in it. The inputs were four warm seasons of 10-minute readings from 16 weather stations (865,928 observations) and nine urban-form features derived from LiDAR and OpenStreetMap. The work was done with the Georgia Tech VIP-SMUR team and is written up as a first-author paper.

Research contributions

  • Problem formulation. Framed forecasting at unmonitored locations as a joint temporal and spatial transfer problem, and scored complete pipelines instead of evaluating each stage in isolation.
  • Evaluation protocol. Designed a 12-fold test that holds out the target station and the target year at the same time, with paired hierarchical bootstrap inference over station and date clusters, plus stress tests for network density and contiguous spatial holdout.
  • Findings. Showed that the stage-wise winners (linear regression, IDW) still have the lowest error end-to-end, that the spatial method moves error more than the temporal model does, and that the neural model's advantage is regime-specific: in an exploratory hottest-decile analysis it lowered six-hour error with a confidence interval excluding zero.
  • Honest limits. Separated the resolution of the output grid from the resolution the station network can validate, and showed why cross-station conformal intervals that look calibrated on average fail at individual locations.

What I built

  • Data pipeline. Gap-aware segmentation and resampling of 10-minute sensor series, sliding-window construction for 24-hour inputs, and scalers fit only on the training partition.
  • Forecasting model. A 337K-parameter PyTorch network (variable selection, parallel LSTM branches, self-attention) that predicts two variables at five horizons in one forward pass, replacing ten separately fitted models.
  • Baseline suite. Persistence, per-target linear regression, Vanilla LSTM, TCN, PatchTST, and TFT, all on the same data pipeline, splits, and horizons.
  • Spatial module. IDW, Ordinary Kriging, and Regression Kriging (Random Forest trend plus kriged residuals) that move station forecasts onto a 100,283-point campus grid.
  • Evaluation harness. Held-out-year, held-out-station, 12-fold end-to-end, and spatial-block protocols, paired hierarchical bootstrap confidence intervals, and an automated leakage and provenance audit.
  • HPC workflow. SLURM jobs for training, inference, and map generation on Georgia Tech's PACE A100 nodes, plus an onboarding guide I wrote for new team members.

Forecast at any campus location

A location without a sensor has no history to forecast from. The system handles this in two stages: forecast at every station that does have data, then transfer those forecasts across space. Keeping the stages separate means each can be swapped and benchmarked on its own, and every temporal model can be paired with every spatial method.

Stage 1: forecasting model

Each sample is 144 timesteps (24 hours) with eight features: temperature, humidity, and sine/cosine encodings of time of day, solar altitude, and solar azimuth. Outputs are temperature and humidity at 10, 30, 60, 180, and 360 minutes.

Variable selection
A gated residual network produces softmax feature weights from the time-averaged input and applies them at every timestep. It is lighter than TFT's per-timestep selection and keeps the weights inspectable; humidity (0.385) and temperature (0.207) receive the most weight.
Parallel LSTM branches
A one-layer and a two-layer LSTM read the same sequence at half the hidden size each, and their outputs are concatenated.
Self-attention
Two-head self-attention with a residual connection and layer norm over the full 24-hour window.
Training
Joint MSE over all ten outputs, AdamW with weight decay, gradient clipping, ReduceLROnPlateau, and early stopping on validation loss. Five random seeds for the full model and each ablation.

Stage 2: spatial transfer

Regression Kriging fits a Random Forest (50 trees, depth 10) on nine features per location: building height, ground and total elevation, distances to the nearest building, park, library, parking lot, and footway, and a monthly shadow ratio computed from building geometry and sun path. The station residuals are then interpolated with Ordinary Kriging on a fixed spherical variogram. IDW and plain Ordinary Kriging run through the same interface. A failed fit is logged as a failure instead of silently falling back to another method, so no result mixes methods.

Evaluation and leakage control

A random split would put near-duplicate 10-minute windows in both train and test, and a test on stations the model has already seen says nothing about a location without a sensor. I set up four protocols, each closer to the real use case:

  • Held-out year. Train on 2015–2016, validate on 2017, test on 2019.
  • Held-out station. Run the trained model on a station excluded from training and scaling.
  • End-to-end (12 folds). Hold out one station and the 2019 season together, refit the temporal models, forecast from the remaining stations, and transfer to the held-out location. The target station supplies no history, no training data, and no scaler statistics.
  • Spatial blocks. Remove four neighboring stations at once to test transfer into a whole unseen region.

The end-to-end run is audited automatically. For every fold, the aggregation script confirms that the held-out station is absent from training, validation, scaler fitting, and source histories; checks each target timestamp against its reference time and horizon; verifies the SHA-256 checksum of every prediction file; and confirms that the data, model, preprocessing, and spatial code hashes are identical across folds. If a fold is missing or a method has been substituted, the run stops. Differences between pipelines are reported with paired hierarchical bootstrap intervals that resample stations and then dates, since consecutive readings are strongly correlated.

Among the neural networks, mine had the lowest six-hour error (3.18 °C against 3.38–3.67 °C). I included a per-target linear regression on the flattened 24-hour history from the start, and it had the lowest error at every horizon for both variables. At 10-minute resolution this signal is strongly autoregressive, and a linear model captures that directly. This matches Zeng et al. (2023), where simple linear models outperformed several Transformer architectures on long-horizon forecasting benchmarks, and it ended up shaping the final recommendation.

Moving from a known station to an unseen one raised 10-minute temperature error from 0.47 to 2.77 °C for the best pipeline, which is why the stricter protocol matters. The spatial method drove most of the difference: replacing IDW with RK added 0.31–0.33 °C at 10–60 minutes, with bootstrap intervals above zero. Between my model and linear regression, the intervals included zero at four of five horizons for temperature and at every horizon for humidity.

Where the neural model wins: six-hour temperature forecasts during heat. In the hottest decile of observed temperatures, my model + IDW cut 360-minute RMSE from 4.88 to 4.59 °C compared with linear regression + IDW (paired difference −0.289 °C, 95% CI −0.450 to −0.103). Six hours of lead time is what planning ahead of a heat event needs, for example building precooling, although those downstream uses were not evaluated here. The subset was defined after the main analysis, so it still needs confirmation on an independent sensor network.

The full model had the lowest seed-to-seed variance at six hours (±0.06 °C versus ±0.08–0.30 °C for the ablations). A single wider LSTM reached lower error with 39% more parameters, so the two-branch design trades a little accuracy for a smaller model. The single-run gains from variable selection and attention fall inside the seed spread, and I report them that way instead of claiming them as wins.

Uncertainty

I also tested whether cross-station conformal prediction could provide forecast intervals. Averaged over stations, coverage came in at 89–90% for a 90% target, which looks fine. Per station, though, coverage ranged from under 50% to nearly 100%, and six-hour temperature intervals were about 12.5 °C wide. An interval that only holds on average across locations is not something a user at a specific location can rely on, so I kept it as a diagnostic and left calibrated per-location uncertainty as open work.

Maps and compute

For the hottest, coldest, driest, and most humid timestamps in the record, the system writes temperature and humidity fields to all 100,283 grid points. The 5.9 m spacing is the output grid; with 16 stations, values between sensors can't be independently checked, so the maps are presented as model output.

Training takes about 23 minutes on one A100 (best validation loss at epoch 3, early stop at 23). A full forecast for one reference time, covering both variables, all five horizons, and the full grid, runs in about 2 minutes 10 seconds, with kriging on CPU.

Implications

For research. Gains reported for deep models on urban microclimate should be checked against linear baselines and evaluated at locations the model has never seen, because both change the ranking here. The useful signal for neural models is regime-specific rather than average: the heat-decile result is where further work should focus. The open problems this leaves are calibrated uncertainty at individual locations, independent validation of between-station gradients (for example with mobile transects), and testing the heat result in other climates and denser urban fabric.

For deployment. If I were deploying this today, I would ship linear regression with IDW as the default forecaster: it had the lowest average error, is cheap to fit and serve, and is easy to debug. I would keep the neural model for one job, six-hour temperature forecasts during heat events, and validate it on a second network before relying on it.

Across both, the main lesson is how much the evaluation setup decides the answer. The same pipeline looks very different at a known station than at an unseen one, and a model can lead the other neural networks and still lose to linear regression. The leakage audit and the stricter protocols are what make these conclusions trustworthy, and I would build them first on the next project.
